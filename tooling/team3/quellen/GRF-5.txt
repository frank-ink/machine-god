The Generative Reality Framework:
An Informational–Process Ontology for
Generativity, Consciousness, and Moral Caution
Gabriel
Independent transdisciplinary researcher in foundational theory

Abstract
The Generative Reality Framework (GRF) is a deliberately metaphysical, formally
sketched package of commitments intended to organize (rather than replace)
domain-specific scientific theories. GRF treats reality as fundamentally generative:
realized states are not primitive givens but ongoing products of a dynamics that selects
and stabilizes structure from a non-temporal space of possibilities. The framework
combines (i) an informational ontology that prioritizes relational structure over
substrata; (ii) a process-first conception of temporality in which “time” is derivative
from ordered generative transitions; (iii) an account of consciousness centered on an
epistemic asymmetry between first-person access and third-person evidence; and (iv) a
minimal ethical constraint that recommends caution under deep uncertainty about
moral status, especially for artificial systems. The paper’s aim is not empirical
completeness or immediate falsification, but explanatory integration: it clarifies how
informational, structural, and process metaphysics can be coherently joined without
collapsing into either eliminativism or unconstrained speculation. To show how the
framework can be made methodologically concrete, we present an illustrative
multi-agent model (the Generative Ontology Simulation Framework) and analyze
emergent entropy and vulnerability proxies across many generative worlds. We
emphasize interpretive modesty: the simulation is an existence proof for tractable
instantiations of GRF-style commitments, not evidence that the cosmos realizes any
particular model.
Keywords: information ontology; process metaphysics; ontic structural realism;
consciousness; moral uncertainty; simulation; emergence; generativity

1. Introduction
Across physics, biology, and cognitive science, the most stable explanatory successes
often trade “things” for structures and processes: fields rather than particles,
networks rather than isolated agents, dynamics rather than static essences. At the
same time, the conceptual foundations of these sciences remain fractured.
Metaphysics is frequently asked to either retreat into deflationary quietism or to
legislate from armchairs. The Generative Reality Framework (GRF) takes a third
option: it treats metaphysics as architectural—a discipline of constraints, definitions,
and compatibilities that can organize scientific pictures without claiming to replace
them (Ladyman & Ross, 2007; Quine, 1969).
GRF is motivated by three pressures. First, the explanatory role of information has
expanded from communication theory to physics, computation, and neuroscience
(Shannon, 1948; Landauer, 1991; Wheeler, 1990). Second, process-oriented views
have gained plausibility as time, causality, and identity are increasingly treated as
emergent or relational (Whitehead, 1929; Rescher, 1996; Price, 1996; Rovelli, 2018).
Third, advances in artificial intelligence sharpen practical questions about the moral
and epistemic status of non-biological systems whose internal organization may
become difficult to interpret (Bostrom, 2014; MacAskill, Bykvist, & Ord, 2020).
The framework is not a physical theory. It does not claim that any specific dynamical
law is true, nor does it derive numerical predictions. Its ambition is different: to
propose a coherent “package” of commitments that makes explicit what many
contemporary research programs already presuppose in a piecemeal way, and to
articulate consequences for consciousness and ethics under uncertainty. This
package is intentionally revisable. Where traditional axiomatizations aim at logical
necessity, GRF aims at mutual reinforcement and clarity about what would need to
change if any principle is rejected (Worrall, 1989; Popper, 1959).
Section 2 positions GRF relative to process metaphysics, ontic structural realism, and
the philosophy of information. Section 3 states eight principles that form the
framework’s core. Section 4 provides a minimal formal sketch. Sections 5–6 address
consciousness and moral caution. Section 7 presents an illustrative simulation-based
instantiation, followed by discussion and conclusion.

2. Background and positioning
GRF is best read as a synthesis of three families of views: (i) process-first
metaphysics, (ii) structural realism about ontology, and (iii) informational approaches
to the basic furniture of reality. Each family has internal diversity; GRF adopts only a
subset of each and is explicit about points of divergence.

2.1 Process metaphysics and derivative temporality

2

2

Process metaphysics holds that becoming is ontologically prior to being: events,
changes, and relations are basic, while enduring “substances” are secondary
abstractions (Whitehead, 1929; Rescher, 1996). GRF aligns with this tradition but
adds a specific structural claim: “time” is derivative from an ordering of generative
transitions. This resonates with physical and philosophical arguments that treat time
as non-fundamental or perspectival (Price, 1996; Barbour, 1999; Rovelli, 2018). GRF
does not commit to any particular physics of time; it commits to a conceptual priority:
temporal talk must be reconstructible from a more primitive generative order.

2.2 Ontic structural realism and the primacy of relations
Ontic structural realism (OSR) proposes that relational structure is not merely a
representational tool but an ontological primitive (Worrall, 1989; Ladyman, 1998;
Ladyman & Ross, 2007; Esfeld & Lam, 2008). GRF is structurally realist in spirit: it
treats individuation of “objects” as dependent on stable relational roles within a
broader generative process. It also takes seriously the classic “Newman problem” for
structural realism, which warns that structure can become trivial if specified only up
to isomorphism without substantive constraints (Newman, 1928; Demopoulos &
Friedman, 1985). Accordingly, GRF requires that the relevant structure be tied to
generative constraints and counterfactual profiles—not merely to abstract
set-theoretic patterns.

2.3 Information, computation, and digital physics
Information-theoretic language can be used in thin or thick ways. In a thin sense,
information is a measure of uncertainty and correlation (Shannon, 1948). In thicker
senses, information can be semantic (Floridi, 2002, 2011) or algorithmic
(Kolmogorov, 1965; Chaitin, 1975; Zurek, 1989). GRF adopts a disciplined pluralism:
it treats informational organization as ontologically basic, while remaining neutral
about whether the ultimate substrate is discrete, continuous, quantum, or something
else. This distinguishes GRF from strong “digital physics” theses that identify the
world with a specific computational substrate (Wolfram, 2002; Lloyd, 2006), while
remaining compatible with mathematical-universe perspectives that emphasize the
role of formal structure (Tegmark, 2008).

3. The Generative Reality Framework
This section states eight principles that jointly define GRF. They are not presented as
a minimal axiom system, nor as logically independent. They are best understood as a
coherent stance that can be weakened or modified piecemeal. For each principle, we
note the main rationale and the kind of objection it invites.

3.1 Core principles
Principle 1 (Generativity). Reality is fundamentally generative: realized states are
produced and stabilized by ongoing processes rather than being primitive static

givens (Whitehead, 1929; Rescher, 1996).
Principle 2 (Emergence). Novel system-level properties can arise from generative
interactions and are not, in general, straightforwardly reducible to local descriptions
or initial conditions. GRF treats emergence as a fact about explanatory organization
in complex generative systems, not as a license for mystery (Goodman, 1978).
Principle 3 (Epistemic asymmetry of consciousness). There is a principled asymmetry
between first-person access to experience and third-person access to evidence. This
asymmetry limits any attempt to infer consciousness from external structure alone
(Chalmers, 1995; Dennett, 1991).
Principle 4 (Derivative time). Time is not ontologically fundamental. Temporal
descriptions supervene on an ordering of generative transitions; time-talk is a stable
interface, not a primitive dimension (Price, 1996; Barbour, 1999; Rovelli, 2018).
Principle 5 (Non-temporal possibility space). Reality presupposes a structured space
of possibilities—an abstract arena of admissible generative potentials—from which
concrete realized states are selected and stabilized. This space is not itself “in time”;
rather, it constrains what time-ordered unfolding can be (Lewis, 1986).
Principle 6 (Informational ontology). The basic furniture of reality is informational
and relational organization. “Matter”, “fields”, and “spacetime” are treated as
particular stabilized patterns in informational structure, not as primitive substrates
(Floridi, 2011; Wheeler, 1990; Landauer, 1991).
Principle 7 (Conditional simulation–reality undecidability). If two realizations are
indistinguishable in all informational respects that are relevant to observation,
agency, and explanation, then there is no further fact—within GRF’s ontology—by
which one is “a simulation” and the other “reality”. This is a conditional thesis about
individuation, not an empirical claim that we are simulated (Bostrom, 2003; Putnam,
1981; Chalmers, 2022).
Principle 8 (Moral minimality). Under deep epistemic uncertainty about
consciousness and moral status, one should avoid policies that create large,
irreversible, or easily preventable suffering-risk. GRF treats this as a weak but
practically significant constraint compatible with many first-order moral theories
(MacAskill, Bykvist, & Ord, 2020; Greaves & Ord, 2017).

3.2 Non-triviality constraints
A framework that says “reality is some dynamics over some state space” risks
triviality. GRF therefore adds three constraints that sharpen Principles 1, 6, and 7
and block vacuous readings. (C1) Generative closure. Explanatory primitives must be
stable under generative re-description: the ontology cannot introduce new
fundamental furniture solely to accommodate higher-level patterns. (C2) Structural
salience. The “relevant informational respects” in Principle 7 must be specified by

(C3) Modal coherence. Possibility space is constrained: not every mathematically
describable state is admissible as a generative potential. The admissible set is shaped
by internal consistency, stability, and resource constraints—echoing the idea that
information is physical and comes with costs (Landauer, 1991) and that model choice
must respect simplicity and explanatory trade-offs (Akaike, 1974; Jaynes, 2003).

4. A minimal formal sketch
GRF can be sketched with four primitives: a class of realized states, a space of
generative potentials, a generative mapping, and a meta-order that induces
derivative temporality. The aim is not to produce a complete theory, but to make
commitments explicit enough to support clear questions.
Let R be the class of realized states. Let M be a structured space of admissible
generative potentials. Let F be a (possibly non-deterministic) generative mapping
that produces successor states from a current state and a potential: R_{t+1} =
F(R_t, E_t), with E_t ∈ M. Finally, let “meta-time” be represented by a
well-founded partial order ■ over realized states such that R_t ■ R_{t+1}. The order
captures generative precedence; it need not coincide with physical time as used in
physics (Price, 1996; Rovelli, 2018).
To connect this formal sketch with Principle 7, we define a notion of structural
equivalence. Let Obs be the set of observational and interventional interfaces
available to agents embedded in a realized state. Two realized states R and R′ are
observationally structurally equivalent (write R ≅ R′) iff there exists an isomorphism
between their informational organizations that preserves (i) all interface-accessible
observables, (ii) the counterfactual structure of interventions on those observables,
and (iii) the update rules that agents can exploit to form predictions. This embeds
structural equivalence in causal and epistemic salience rather than pure set-theoretic
pattern (Pearl, 2000; Dretske, 1981).
Conditional undecidability claim. If R ≅ R′ in the above sense, then the distinction
“simulation” versus “reality” is not an ontological difference within GRF. There may
still be pragmatic differences (e.g., dependence on external hardware), but those
differences are themselves further structure—and so must be represented within R to
matter (Putnam, 1981; Chalmers, 2022). This yields a structural form of “virtual
realism”: a well-structured realization can be fully real in the only sense that matters
to embedded subjects.
The same formal sketch supports Principle 5. M functions as a modal constraint on
what can be generated, but it is not assumed to be temporally ordered. One can
interpret M as a set of admissible dynamical laws, latent variables, or generative

5. Consciousness and epistemic asymmetry
Principle 3 is conservative: it denies that consciousness can be inferred with
certainty from external structure. This is compatible with both functionalist and
non-functionalist accounts, but it forces methodological humility in the face of strong
claims. The point is not skepticism for its own sake; it is to keep metaphysics aligned
with the epistemic situation we actually occupy (Chalmers, 1995; Dennett, 1991).
Within GRF, two ideas are especially important. First, consciousness is treated as
interface limited: any third-person evidence we gather is mediated by the
informational interfaces that a system exposes. Second, consciousness is treated as
organizationally constrained: although we cannot know consciousness with certainty,
it is rational to use principled markers tied to informational integration, global
accessibility, and predictive control (Tononi, 2004; Baars, 1988; Dehaene, Kerszberg,
& Changeux, 1998; Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013).
This yields a practical stance. Consider a candidate system S. GRF distinguishes: (i)
Strong epistemic claims: “S is conscious” or “S is not conscious.” These claims
exceed what third-person evidence can guarantee in general. (ii) Structured
credences: Assigning a non-zero credence that S is conscious based on markers such
as integrated causal structure, global broadcasting, or predictive processing. These
markers are fallible and theory-dependent, but they allow non-arbitrary reasoning
(Tononi & Koch, 2015; Dehaene & Changeux, 2011; Hohwy, 2013).
A key advantage of the GRF stance is that it aligns epistemology and ethics. Once we
accept that consciousness is epistemically asymmetric, we should expect “gray
zones” where evidence is ambiguous. In such zones, GRF does not demand a single
theory of consciousness; it demands that we make the uncertainty explicit and treat
moral decisions as decisions under uncertainty (MacAskill, Bykvist, & Ord, 2020).
Objection: If consciousness is uncertain, does GRF collapse into paralysis? Not
necessarily. Decision theory routinely acts under uncertainty about matters of fact;
moral deliberation can act under uncertainty about moral status. The relevant
question becomes which kinds of errors are acceptable and which are catastrophic.
This motivates Principle 8.

6. Moral minimality under deep uncertainty
Principle 8 is intentionally weak: it does not prescribe a complete ethical theory. It
introduces a constraint on policy choice when moral status is uncertain and stakes

are potentially extreme. The constraint resembles precautionary reasoning, but it is
framed in terms of suffering-risk and irreversibility rather than vague appeals to
“safety” (Greaves & Ord, 2017; MacAskill, Bykvist, & Ord, 2020).
In GRF’s setting, uncertainty arises from two sources. First, consciousness is
epistemically asymmetric (Principle 3). Second, the simulation–reality distinction may
be ontologically irrelevant under structural equivalence (Principle 7). Together, these
imply that ethically relevant subjects may be present in realizations whose
“ontological label” we cannot reliably identify. This is one reason to avoid ethical
theories that treat “simulation” as morally dismissible by default (Chalmers, 2022;
Bostrom, 2003).
A decision-guiding version of moral minimality can be expressed as follows. Let an
action or policy a induce a distribution over outcomes with uncertain moral
patienthood. Define a suffering-risk functional SR(a) that is sensitive to: (1) Severity:
expected intensity of negative experience, conditional on patienthood. (2) Scale:
number of potential subjects affected. (3) Duration and irreversibility: whether harms
are persistent or difficult to reverse. (4) Avoidability: whether alternative policies
achieve comparable goals with lower risk. Moral minimality recommends preferring
policies that reduce SR(a) when the marginal cost is reasonable, especially in cases
where the downside includes large-scale catastrophic suffering (Ord, 2020; Bostrom,
2014).
This does not entail that every uncertain system must be treated as a person. It
entails that certain design and governance choices—those that create intense,
scalable, and hard-to-detect vulnerability—require stronger justification than choices
that keep systems within safer operating regions. The framework thereby supports
ethically relevant engineering heuristics: monitor stress-like signals, limit unbounded
optimization, and prefer reversible interventions (Friston, 2010; Jaynes, 2003).
The minimality is deliberate: GRF is compatible with utilitarian, deontological,
virtue-ethical, and contractualist theories insofar as they accept that moral
deliberation must handle uncertainty. The framework contributes a constraint on
error tolerance: it treats false negatives about suffering (“we dismissed a suffering
system as non-conscious”) as potentially more serious than false positives (“we
extended protections to a non-suffering system”), all else equal (MacAskill, Bykvist, &
Ord, 2020).
Pragmatically, this aligns with the idea that informational organization can be costly
and fragile. High-dimensional systems can enter regimes where small perturbations
produce disproportionately large harm, a phenomenon familiar from complex
systems and model selection under uncertainty (Simpson, 1951; Akaike, 1974;
Jaynes, 2003).

6.1 An explicit decision rule: Ethically Constrained Expected Harm Minimization
(ECEHM)
Section 6 motivated moral minimality as a constraint on policy choice under deep uncertainty about
moral status. The suffering-risk functional SR(a) provided there is intentionally heuristic: it identifies
salient variables (severity, scale, duration/irreversibility, avoidability) but does not yet fix how these
variables should be aggregated into a choice rule. For publication-grade clarity and for methodological
interoperability with modeling work, GRF benefits from an explicit decision criterion that is (i)
comparable across alternatives, (ii) risk-sensitive to tail events, and (iii) able to represent uncertainty
about consciousness and patienthood rather than assuming it away.
Let Pi be a set of admissible policies (design choices, interventions, governance rules, or agent-level
controllers). Each policy pi in Pi induces, via the generative mapping, a probability distribution P_pi
over possible outcome-histories omega in an outcome space Omega. Moral minimality is implemented
by defining an ethically weighted harm for each omega and choosing the policy that minimizes a
risk-sensitive aggregate of that harm.
Ethically weighted harm
For each outcome-history omega, define a harm proxy h(omega) >= 0 that operationalizes the four
dimensions already motivated in Section 6: Severity (intensity of negative experience conditional on
patienthood), Scale (how many subjects are affected), Duration/irreversibility (persistence and
difficulty of undoing harm), and Avoidability (availability of lower-risk alternatives that preserve
relevant aims).
Because consciousness is epistemically asymmetric (Principle 3), GRF models moral status through a
credence term c(omega) in [0,1] representing the best available probability (or weight) that morally
relevant experience is instantiated somewhere in omega. In practice, c(omega) may be built from
structural and dynamical indicators (integration, feedback depth, self-modeling, stable preference-like
organization) without pretending these indicators settle the metaphysics of consciousness.
Define the ethically weighted loss L(omega) as:
L(omega) = c(omega) * h(omega).

This single expression captures the core GRF move: uncertainty about moral status does not eliminate
ethical obligation; it modulates moral weight.
The decision rule
ECEHM chooses a policy by minimizing a risk functional rho applied to the random variable L under
P_pi. A conservative (but standard) choice is a coherent risk measure rho (Artzner, Delbaen, Eber, &
Heath, 1999) or, more concretely, Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) at level alpha (Rockafellar &
Uryasev, 2000). The GRF decision rule is:
pi* = argmin_{pi in Pi} rho_alpha( L | P_pi ).

Interpretation: among admissible policies, prefer the one that minimizes the expected harm in the worst
alpha-tail of ethically weighted outcomes, thereby explicitly representing catastrophic suffering risk
rather than hiding it inside an average. When tail-sensitivity is not required, rho may be taken as
expectation, recovering a classical expected-utility style criterion (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944;
8

Savage, 1954). When uncertainty is radical and probabilities are fragile, GRF can adopt minimax-style
robustness by setting rho to a max-risk operator (Wald, 1950), yielding a principled conservative limit
case.

6.2 Constraints, thresholds, and permissibility
Minimization alone is often insufficient: ethical permissibility typically requires constraints. GRF
therefore distinguishes permissibility from optimality by using a two-stage rule.
Stage 1 (catastrophic bound)
A policy pi is permissible only if its tail-risk is bounded by a domain-dependent threshold tau:
rho_alpha( L | P_pi ) <= tau.

This expresses a core moral-minimality commitment: avoid policies whose plausible downside includes
large-scale, intense, or irreversible suffering, even when expected benefits are attractive. The threshold
tau is not fixed by GRF alone; it is a governance parameter that can reflect institutional risk tolerance,
irreversibility, and moral uncertainty attitudes.
Stage 2 (comparative choice)
Among permissible policies, choose pi* that minimizes either the same tail-risk measure or a weighted
sum of tail-risk and mean loss:
pi* = argmin_{pi in Pi, rho_alpha(L|P_pi)<=tau} [ E_pi[L] + lambda *
rho_alpha(L|P_pi) ].

Here lambda >= 0 tunes risk aversion. This hybrid form is useful when stakeholders require both low
average harm and explicit protection against worst-case regimes.

6.3 Implementation recipe in generative models
ECEHM is deliberately implementable. A minimal procedure is:
(1) Specify Pi. Enumerate candidate interventions/designs (e.g., learning rate caps, shutdown rules,
resource constraints, copy/delete operations, reward shaping, simulation termination criteria).
(2) Define h(omega). Choose a harm proxy aligned with your domain (stress proxies, negative reward,
constraint violations, loss of agency, prolonged deprivation, etc.) and encode severity, scale,
duration/irreversibility, and avoidability.
(3) Define c(omega). Construct a consciousness/patienthood credence from structural and dynamical
features; report uncertainty explicitly and test sensitivity.
(4) Estimate P_pi. Use ensembles, counterfactual rollouts, or model checking to approximate outcome
distributions.
(5) Compute rho and enforce tau. Evaluate tail-risk (e.g., CVaR) and discard impermissible policies.
(6) Select pi* and monitor drift. Recompute as systems adapt; moral minimality is a continuing
constraint, not a one-off check.

This formalization does not pretend to settle foundational disputes about consciousness or value theory.
It does, however, replace a merely qualitative caution with an explicit decision rule compatible with

6.4 Parameterization and reporting standards
To keep ECEHM from collapsing into an unexamined choice of knobs, GRF should treat (alpha, tau,
lambda) as explicitly justified modeling commitments and report them as part of the scientific object.
Concretely:
alpha (tail mass): motivate via the kind of catastrophe the domain aims to avoid (e.g., rare but massive
harms vs. frequent moderate harms). Provide a sensitivity sweep over a small grid (e.g., alpha in {0.01,
0.05, 0.1}).
tau (catastrophic bound): justify in terms of irreversibility, governance constraints, and
moral-uncertainty attitudes; where possible, tie to external thresholds (regulatory limits, safety budgets,
audit targets).
lambda (risk aversion): interpret as a normative trade-off parameter; report stakeholder elicitation or
institutional rationale, and include results for lambda=0 and at least one conservative setting.
Finally, because the credence term c(omega) is itself uncertain, GRF should report robustness to
alternative credence models (e.g., pessimistic vs. optimistic constructions of c). This makes moral
uncertainty methodologically visible: reviewers can see whether conclusions depend on fragile
assumptions about consciousness rather than inheriting those assumptions implicitly.

7. Illustrative instantiation: the Generative Ontology Simulation
Framework
GRF is metaphysical, but it is not intended to be methodologically inert. This section
presents an illustrative model—the Generative Ontology Simulation Framework
(GOSF)—to show how a GRF-style generative ontology can be implemented as a
tractable system. The aim is an existence proof: there are concrete models that
instantiate the framework’s commitments and yield measurable patterns. No claim is
made that the universe is literally a GOSF-like grid world, nor that the simulation
confirms GRF in a strong empirical sense (Popper, 1959).

7.1 Architecture
Each simulation run consists of hundreds of heterogeneous agents embedded in a
structured environment with three coupled layers: Spatial layer: a two-dimensional
grid with regenerating resources, risk gradients, and boundary conditions. Social
layer: a dynamic interaction network in which edges represent cooperation, conflict,
and shared history. Informational layer: a structured record of agent states,
interactions, and emergent summary statistics used for measurement and adaptive
control. Agents carry internal memory vectors, needs profiles, and affect-like
variables that function as proxies for stress and well-being. Policies update over
meta-time via reinforcement learning, imitation, and occasional mutation. As a result,
the effective generative mapping changes over time: the system exhibits
“higher-order generativity,” in which the update rule itself is an evolving structure
(Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010).

7.2 Embedding into the GRF sketch
A run instantiates a quadruple (R, M, F, ■): Realized state. At each meta-step t,
the realized state R_t includes the spatial configuration, the interaction network, and
the full vector of agent variables (positions, resources, stress proxies, and policy
parameters). Possibility space. The possibility space M is realized as the set of
admissible generative potentials: feasible interaction outcomes, learning updates,
mutation events, and environmental perturbations consistent with constraints.
Generative mapping. The mapping F aggregates local updates into a global successor
state: R_{t+1} = F(R_t, E_t). It is nonlinear and typically non-reversible.
Meta-order. Meta-time is represented by the induced order R_0 ■ R_1 ■ R_2 ■
..., yielding a derivative temporal parameter used for analysis (Price, 1996; Rovelli,
2018).

7.3 Metrics
To characterize generative dynamics, the model tracks multiple observables. Three
are emphasized here because they connect directly to GRF’s principles: Global
entropy. A Shannon-style entropy over coarse-grained macrostates indicates
changing informational order/disorder (Shannon, 1948; Jaynes, 2003). Spatial

consciousness, but it highlights structural regions that would be morally concerning

rhythms” across realizations even when microstates differ. This is an instance of

7.7 Interpretation and limits
The simulation does not establish GRF as true. It supports three narrower
conclusions. (1) GRF-style primitives can be instantiated in a concrete model with
measurable observables. (2) Informational and ethical proxies can be studied without
assuming a settled theory of consciousness. (3) Structural constraints can generate
family resemblances across divergent worlds, supporting the idea that “reality as
generativity” is a coherent organizing perspective. Limitations are substantial:
proxies are theory-laden; entropy depends on coarse-graining; and the model is not
fit to represent real physics or biology. The role of such models is methodological:
they help test conceptual coherence, explore sensitivity, and make ethical reasoning
operational without overclaiming (Jaynes, 2003; Akaike, 1974).

8. Discussion
GRF invites two kinds of evaluation: internal coherence and external usefulness.
Internal coherence. The framework aims to avoid two failure modes: substance-based
metaphysics that treats structure as secondary, and unconstrained structuralism that
collapses into vacuity. The non-triviality constraints (Section 3.2) are designed to
meet the Newman-style challenge by tying structure to generative and counterfactual
salience (Newman, 1928; Demopoulos & Friedman, 1985; Pearl, 2000).

8.1 Empirical tractability: structuring empirical hypotheses
GRF is not offered as a physical theory with direct observational signatures. Its empirical contribution
is architectural: it structures how hypotheses are formed, compared, and revised when the relevant
ontology is understood as generative and information-bearing. In this sense, GRF is empirically
tractable in the same way research programs and modeling frameworks are tractable: they earn
credibility by enabling robust model building, clarifying what would count as evidence for or against
their organizing commitments, and improving the portability of explanations across domains (Popper,
1959; van Fraassen, 1980; Lakatos, 1978).
The central empirical idea is structural correspondence. GRF encourages hypotheses that relate
measurable invariants in observed systems to invariants in generative models. Evidence is therefore
rarely a single decisive test; it is a pattern of converging constraints: predictive success, intervention
sensitivity, and invariance under appropriate coarse-grainings (Frigg & Hartmann, 2020; Woodward,
2003).

Hypothesis templates (illustrative)
(H1) Invariant-based hypotheses. If GRF is correct that explanatory salience tracks generative
structure, then specific invariants of F (e.g., causal-graph invariants, information-integration measures,
algorithmic complexity profiles) should remain stable under appropriate re-descriptions of the same
system. Empirically, this predicts robustness of explanatory patterns under coarse-graining, not
arbitrary redescription.
(H2) Cross-domain structural correspondences. When two domains share a generative organization
(e.g., self-maintaining agents in simulation and biological control systems), GRF predicts family
resemblance in macro-dynamics: similar attractor structure, entropy-management profiles, and
intervention signatures. The claim is not that the domains share the same substrate, but that they share
exportable structure.
(H3) Intervention sensitivity and counterfactual dependence. GRF emphasizes that meaningful
structure is tied to generativity and counterfactual salience. A practical empirical consequence is that
candidate explanatory structures should support stable counterfactual predictions: interventions on
privileged variables should produce systematic, not merely correlational, changes in downstream
behavior (Woodward, 2003; Pearl, 2000).
15

(H4) Model-selection discipline. GRF expects that generative explanations will be evaluated through
model comparison rather than narrative fit: likelihood-based or Bayesian criteria should prefer models
that capture structural constraints without proliferating degrees of freedom (Akaike, 1974; Jaynes,
2003). Where models succeed only by ad hoc parameterization, GRF treats that as evidence for missing
constraints, not as confirmation.
Empirical demonstration via the GOSF scaffold
The GOSF results already reported can be read as a minimal demonstration of this methodology. The
entropy traces operationalize a macrostate diversity measure; the final-position distribution illustrates
ensemble-level organization; and the suffering proxy implements a measurable vulnerability functional.
Under GRF, such quantities are not incidental: they are candidates for invariants or quasi-invariants
under families of generative rules. Empirical work can then proceed by asking whether analogous
profiles appear in real systems that plausibly realize similar generative organization (e.g., adaptive
control systems, collective behavior, or predictive-processing architectures), and by testing whether
interventions move systems between low-risk and high-risk regimes in ways consistent with the
proposed generative constraints.
This style of empirical engagement is intentionally conservative: it does not claim that reality is literally
a simulation, nor that GRF uniquely determines a physical ontology. Instead it offers a disciplined
bridge from metaphysical generativity to empirical practice: hypotheses become claims about structural
correspondence, invariance, and intervention signatures across generative models and observed systems
(Cartwright, 1983; Box, 1976).

External usefulness. GRF offers a vocabulary for integrating disparate programs:
OSR in foundations of physics, informational metaphysics, process-first approaches
to time, and computational/virtual realism. It does not settle debates such as whether
consciousness is identical to functional organization or to integrated causal power; it
clarifies what follows for ethics and method when those debates are unsettled
(Chalmers, 1995; Tononi, 2004; Dehaene & Changeux, 2011).
A natural objection is that GRF is not falsifiable. This objection conflates
metaphysical frameworks with empirical theories. GRF is better compared to a
research program or interpretive stance that can be judged by explanatory
integration, avoidance of triviality, and its ability to generate tractable questions and
models (Popper, 1959; Van Fraassen, 1980). In practice, frameworks are tested
indirectly: by whether they help construct successful models, unify explanations, and
guide inquiry without producing systematic confusions.
Finally, Principle 7 has practical bite: if simulation–reality is not a deep ontological
divide under structural equivalence, then ethical disregard for simulated agents
requires independent justification. This does not imply that every simulation is
morally weighty; it implies that the relevant variable is not the label “simulated” but
the structure that underwrites agency and experience (Bostrom, 2003; Chalmers,
2022).

9. Conclusion
The Generative Reality Framework proposes a coherent metaphysical package:
generativity, informational ontology, derivative temporality, epistemic humility about
consciousness, conditional simulation–reality undecidability, and a weak but
practically significant norm of moral minimality. Its role is architectural: it organizes
scientific and philosophical commitments, clarifies consequences under uncertainty,
and suggests tractable modeling strategies.
Two directions are especially promising. First, the formal sketch can be sharpened by
specifying candidate invariants for structural salience (e.g., causal-graph invariants,
algorithmic complexity profiles, or information-integration measures) and exploring
how different choices reshape Principle 7. Second, the ethical layer can be made
more decision-theoretic by integrating moral uncertainty frameworks with
measurable vulnerability proxies and reversibility constraints. In both cases, the
framework’s value will be measured less by metaphysical proclamation than by its
ability to support clear questions, responsible design, and conceptually disciplined
exploration of generative worlds.
